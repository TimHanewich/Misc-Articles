# Fulfillment in Struggle
![img](https://i.imgur.com/NocjxgA.jpeg)

Growing up in Western culture, I've seen how heavily *productivity* and *hustle* can be instilled from such an early age. That emphasis has roots stretching back generations:
- Religions like Protestantism viewed work as a "saving grace" that might lead the common person to salvation.
- The Industrial Revolution introduced the time clock, making time a measured unit of production.
- Management science in the 90s began treating the labor force as a resource to optimize for maximum productivity.

In terms of economic output, this has served the US well! This productivity-focused culture helped create one of the world's most prosperous economies, establishing the US as a superpower despite its relative youth compared with historical superpowers.

A prosperous economy brings many benefits (better quality of life, healthcare, safety, abundant nutrition, etc.), but it can also have side effects.

As early as elementary school, children are taught to pursue good grades to qualify for advanced placement classes in high school. High school students are pushed to score well on placement exams to enter the best universities. University students are pushed to secure Fortune 500 internships to improve their chances of a lucrative career. New graduates are encouraged to work long hours to earn promotions. Those promoted are encouraged to take leadership positions for greater impact. Those in leadership are encouraged to climb further and lead entire organizations.

This "hustle" mentality is rooted in the developed world's culture and reinforced across generations. While this mindset has produced one of the world's most prosperous nations, it has also tightly tied human identity and worth to the productivity and success that innate abilities and hard-earned skills make possible.

## THE DISRUPTION: AI
Since the dawn of civilization, human effort, both labor and intellect, has driven economic development. Over time, capital (machinery) entered the mix and automated much of that labor. This has increasingly shifted human work toward information economy ("white collar") jobs, where intellectual output is the valuable contribution.

For decades, as machines took over physical labor, human workers moved toward "higher ground", where intellect was the product they delivered. True intelligence was always beyond a machine's reach, making intellectual pursuits a refuge of security and value.

**That has now changed.** Artificial Intelligence has proven capable of participating directly in the information economy and serving in many roles humans hold today.

For the first time in history, *critical thinking* can be delegated to a machine. Human cognition is no longer the *only* source of intellect. Moreover, this machine-delivered intelligence is faster, deeper, cheaper, and more widely available. In a capitalist economy, this will drive massive shifts as the cost and time required to deliver intelligence fall dramatically before our eyes.

This will have massive implications for the economy and the future job market. Economists much smarter than me are forecasting what the coming decades might look like (probably with AI's assistance!). In this post, I don't want to pontificate about the economy's future, but about what this change might bring to the human spirit.

## THE PROBLEM
As mentioned earlier, Western culture emphasizes productivity and intellectual achievement so heavily that they often become tied to self-worth and purpose. With abundant intelligence available at a fraction of the cost and time required by humans, capitalist economies will begin to reduce their reliance on human intellect for many tasks, first out of a desire for efficiency (profit) and later out of necessity. If this happens at scale, millions of white collar workers worldwide could be separated from work they spent decades mastering and building an identity around. For most, this will be a rough wake-up call.

According to Pew Research, a [2016 study](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2016/10/06/3-how-americans-view-their-jobs/) found that 51% of Americans derive a sense of identity from their jobs, rising to 66% in STEM fields. That identity may slowly be pried away as AI performs these roles' technical tasks more efficiently and cheaply.

Resistance to these changes is already appearing across many industries.

As I write this, mathematicians are struggling to accept the changes brought by recent advances and discoveries from OpenAI's latest models:

In March 2026, the [Communication Workers of America argued that unions are necessary](https://cwa-union.org/sites/default/files/2026-03/cwanews-2026-spring.pdf) against AI to retain worker value.

These are just three recent examples of resistance to AI-driven changes. We are still in AI's early days, four years after ChatGPT brought the technology to the masses and sparked the wave of investment and discovery now called ["The AI Boom"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_boom). AI will continue rippling through the global economy, eventually reaching every area.

## THE RISK THIS POSES
Many predict a future "utopia" in which AI drives > 95% of the economy and human work becomes optional. In this theoretical situation, humans may receive Universal Basic Income (UBI) checks simply for existing, funded by heavy taxation of AI-performed economic activity combined with proceeds from a "sovereign wealth fund" representing public ownership of AI infrastructure or capital.

For many, getting paid to do nothing while endlessly watching a favorite show with a glass of red wine sounds appealing, but I believe the enjoyment would be short-lived.

In a zoo, animals receive food, shelter, healthcare, and protection without having to earn them. Captivity removes the challenges of foraging, hunting, escaping predators, and similar activities. Although this may seem like a utopia for animals otherwise facing the risks of the wild, it is far from one.

Zoo animals are unhappy even with everything provided for free. They become depressed. Yet in the wild, where they struggle and face death around every corner, they thrive.

[tiger](https://i.imgur.com/s8pxdiu.png)

The first zoo dates to 3500 BCE, based on animal remains archaeologists discovered in Egypt that appear to indicate captivity. In the more than 5,000 years since, captive animals' psychological well-being has been a serious problem for zoo operators!

Many zoo animals in this "utopia" suffer from depression, boredom, and an inability to influence their surroundings through their own efforts. For centuries, veterinarians and animal behaviorists have studied this and introduced artificial challenges and threats: puzzle feeders, hidden food, new objects, varied terrain, and stimulating activities. These efforts have modestly improved captive animals' psychological well-being.

It is striking that a "utopia" providing every need without effort can offer less fulfillment than an environment where every meal must be fought for, an injury may mean death, and a predator may be ready to pounce.

Something in these animals is wired to find satisfaction and fulfillment in "the struggle" itself.

This isn't limited to other animals; humans have faced a similar issue in our physical lives for decades! The Industrial Revolution and subsequent years brought widespread machinery to meet the economy's physical demands. As a result, our bodies have grown weaker and atrophied. But we invented a solution! The gym: artificial physical work that satisfies our animal need to maintain our bodies and supports our psychological happiness.

The point isn't that we need suffering or insecurity. It's that we need opportunities to choose challenges, develop abilities, and exercise agency.

Our modern information economy is the equivalent of hunting, foraging, building shelter, and other activities that fulfill a wild animal's life. It has become our way of staying intellectually active and finding purpose and meaning.

Machinery removed the need for human *physical* labor. AI that reasons and thinks may reduce economic demand for some forms of human *intellectual* work. We may be moving toward a future where applying ourselves becomes optional, with rewards we once worked toward (money, housing, etc.) provided automatically through UBI funded by AI's gains: a "zoo" of sorts.

Where will this leave us? In a Western world obsessed with productivity and an identity tied to intellectual output, entering "a zoo" where effort is no longer required will be shattering for many.

Beyond "the what" described above, I've recently been pondering "the why." Why does the absence of challenges and struggle pose such an existential burden for humans? Two reasons come to mind:
1. We are wired that way as animals. Evolution has "wired" our brains' chemical receptors to reward us when we "do a good job." We face a challenge and solve a problem. It feels good.
2. Western culture, increasingly global, ties self-worth and identity to intellectual output. Our intelligence is a tool for accomplishment. With nothing left to accomplish through our own abilities, we lose our sense of self-value.

## THE PATH AHEAD
As some technical skills potentially become less economically valuable, where does this leave human labor? Time will tell whether humans can turn to "higher ground" (e.g. greater emphasis on human-to-human interaction), but meanwhile, millions of workers employed for their technical prowess will need to reassess their investments in learning and effort. This may be a bitter pill to swallow.

**Detach identity from productivity**. As mentioned above, Western culture heavily ties worth to economic productivity, often rooted in specialized skills that took years to acquire. A first step is to undo this: to *decouple* identity from productivity. This adjustment will likely occur at both individual and generational levels, through personal emotional struggles and broader cultural changes. Three generations from now, writing code or analyzing data may be viewed like bragging today about how quickly one washes clothes with a washboard!

**Prioritize fulfillment over achievement**. Particularly over the past decade, consumerism and social media have fueled a "keeping up with the Joneses" mentality: larger houses, more luxurious cars, the latest electronics, and the finest clothes. These often depend on career achievements that provide the means to acquire them. As AI plays a growing role in the global economy, we must refocus on what matters most: fulfillment and enjoyment of life. If the "utopia" explored earlier comes to pass, with AI driving most of the economy and meeting human needs, our most important task will be finding fulfillment. Develop opinions and taste. Value art for your enjoyment, not because it is "the best." Value a friend's guitar playing not for its precise timing, but because it is human-made and has "soul."

**Self-reflect**. In recent years, humanity has made what may be one of its greatest discoveries. It may even be the last major discovery we ever have to make on our own. Machines can now perform tasks involving *thinking* and *reasoning*, potentially moving us toward much greater intellectual abundance. We are in unprecedented times, and past generations' "playbook" for a successful, fulfilling life has been thrown out. Whatever the future brings, it is largely up to you to decide what to make of it. Humanity may soon have an opportunity to pause and be still, no longer tending to an ever-growing list of workplace demands. In these moments, self-reflection will become more important as you consider how best to handle this opportunity. Some will lack answers and struggle, sinking into depression, unprepared for the loss of identity that an irrelevant skillset may bring. Others may thrive, planning to apply themselves differently but just as meaningfully.
